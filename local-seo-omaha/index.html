What Is Local SEO, and How Is It Different From Regular SEO?

Local SEO targets searches where the person's location changes the answer. Regular SEO competes on relevance and authority alone. Local SEO adds proximity, plus a completely separate result block — the map pack — that's fed by your Google Business Profile rather than your website.

The practical difference: you're managing two properties at once, your site and your profile, and the profile often matters more.

What is the map pack, exactly?

It's the block of three business listings with a map that appears near the top of local searches. Three slots. Not ten. For a search like "plumber near me" in Omaha, those three listings sit above the organic results and take a disproportionate share of the clicks, because they carry a phone number, hours, directions and a star rating right there in the result.

You don't rank in it with your website. You rank in it with your Google Business Profile, which Google evaluates using different inputs than it uses for your pages. That's the single most important thing most business owners don't know.

Do "near me" searches actually need the words "near me"?

No, and this trips people up constantly. Google treats almost any commercial search with obvious local intent as a local search, whether or not anyone typed a city or "near me." Search "emergency plumber" from a phone in Elkhorn and you'll get Elkhorn-area plumbers, because Google inferred what you meant.

That means you don't need to stuff "near me" anywhere. You need to be an obviously local option for the thing being searched. The phrase is a symptom of local intent, not the cause of it.

What happened to local results when AI Overviews arrived?

The page got taller and the competition for attention got worse. On many local queries there's now an AI-generated summary sitting above the ads, which sit above the map pack, which sits above the organic results. Someone looking for a fast answer may never scroll past the first block.

What's interesting is that the businesses being named in those AI summaries are largely the same ones doing local SEO properly. A complete profile, consistent information everywhere, real reviews and a clear website are exactly what makes a business legible to a language model. The work overlaps almost entirely.

Is local SEO worth it if I'm not a storefront?

Usually more so. Service-area businesses — contractors, mobile repair, home services, in-home care — live or die on local search because nobody is walking past your building and remembering you. There's no foot traffic to fall back on.

Google supports this properly: a service-area business can hide its address and instead publish the list of cities it covers. That's the correct configuration for most trades, and a surprising number of Omaha contractors have it set up wrong, which quietly limits how far their listing reaches.

One search, four separate competitions. Each block is won a different way, and your website only directly controls one of them. Businesses that treat local search as "get my site ranked" are competing for the block furthest down the page.

How Google Decides

How Does Google Decide Who Shows Up in the Omaha Map Pack?

Google publishes the answer: relevance, distance, and prominence. Relevance is how well your profile and categories match what was searched. Distance is how far you are from the searcher or the place they named. Prominence is how well known your business is across the web — reviews, links, citations and mentions.

Two of those three are yours to influence. The middle one isn't, which is why no single location dominates an entire metro.

01

Relevance

How closely your profile matches the search. This is decided mostly by your primary and secondary categories, the services you've listed, the description you wrote, and whether your website backs up what the profile claims.

Most under-performing profiles fail here for a boring reason: the primary category is wrong or too broad. A business listed as "Contractor" competes against everything. Listed as "Roofing Contractor," it competes for roofing.

You control this
02

Distance

How far you are from wherever the searcher is standing, or from the place they typed. This is the factor people fight hardest against and can least change, and it's the reason your rankings differ between Millard and Bennington.

The honest framing: distance sets the shape of your reachable area. Prominence sets how far out from your pin you can still win. You widen the circle. You don't move it.

You can't change this
03

Prominence

How well known and well referenced your business is. Reviews are part of it. So are links from other websites, mentions in local news and directories, and how consistently your business information appears across the internet.

This is where the real work lives, and it's the factor that lets a business outrank a closer competitor. A well-known business two miles away routinely beats an anonymous one across the street.

Why does the rate matter more than the total?

Because a business with 300 reviews that stopped collecting them in 2023 reads as a business in decline. A business with 60 reviews adding four a month reads as a business that's busy right now. Recency is visible on every listing — anyone can see the dates, including Google.

Steady beats sudden every time. A burst of forty reviews in one week is the exact signature of a business that bought them, and the platforms are good at spotting it.

What's the right way to ask?

At the moment the work is finished and the customer is visibly happy, in person, and then followed by a text with a direct link. Not a mass email blast three weeks later. Not a QR code on an invoice nobody reads.

Two rules that matter legally and practically: never offer anything in exchange for a review, and never filter who you ask based on how you think they'll rate you. Both are prohibited, both are detectable, and both put your listing at risk for a short-term gain.

What do you do about a bad review?

Reply, calmly, once, in public. Prospects read the response more carefully than the complaint — a measured reply to an unfair review often does more for you than another five-star would.

Don't argue, don't reveal customer details, and don't demand removal unless it genuinely violates policy. A perfect five-star average with no negatives at all actually reads as suspicious to experienced buyers. A 4.7 with thoughtful responses reads as real.

Do reviews on other sites count?

For the map pack specifically, Google reviews carry the most direct weight. But reviews on Facebook, Yelp, BBB, Angi and industry-specific platforms feed prominence more broadly, and they show up when someone searches your business name to check you out before calling.

The practical approach: concentrate the ask on Google, and don't ignore the others when they arrive. A business with reviews in only one place looks thinner than one with a presence across several.

The Profile

What Does a Fully Optimized Google Business Profile Look Like?

Complete in every field, updated regularly, and matching your website exactly. That means correct primary and secondary categories, every service listed individually, accurate service areas, hours including holidays, recent photos, answered questions, and a description that describes the business rather than selling it.

Most profiles we audit are somewhere around forty percent filled in. That gap is usually the cheapest ranking gain available.

✓

The right primary category

This one field does more than anything else on the profile. Pick the most specific category that describes your main service, then add secondary categories for everything else you do. "Roofing Contractor" beats "Contractor" every time.

✓

Every service listed separately

The services section lets you name each thing you do with its own short description. Most profiles leave it empty. It's a direct relevance signal and it takes an afternoon to fill in properly.

✓

Service areas set correctly

If you go to customers, list the cities you actually serve and hide the address. Listing twenty cities you rarely reach doesn't help; listing the ones you genuinely cover does. Google caps how many you can add for a reason.

✓

Hours, including holidays

Wrong hours cost you real calls and quietly damage trust. Set special hours before every holiday. If you offer 24-hour emergency service, say so — it's a common tiebreaker in a category like plumbing.

✓

Real photos, added regularly

Job sites, crews, trucks, finished work. Phone photos are fine — better than stock, in fact. A profile receiving new photos monthly signals an active business; one with four images from 2021 signals the opposite.

✓

Questions answered — including your own

Anyone can post a question to your profile, and anyone can answer it, which means a competitor or a confused stranger might. You're allowed to post and answer your own common questions. Do it before someone else does.

✓

A description that describes

750 characters stating what you do, where, and what makes you a sensible choice. Not adjectives. This text gets read by people comparing three listings and by models deciding whether they understand your business.

✓

Posts, at least monthly

Updates, offers and events appear on your listing and signal activity. One useful post a month beats a burst of ten and then nothing. A finished job with two sentences and a photo qualifies.

✓

Messaging turned on — if you'll answer it

Enabled messaging with nobody watching it is worse than messaging turned off, because slow replies are visible. Turn it on only if someone genuinely checks it during business hours.

✓

Matching your website exactly

Name, phone, hours and services should be identical on the profile and the site. Contradictions between the two are one of the quietest ways to undermine both, and almost nobody checks.

The Boring Half

Why Does Your Phone Number Being Wrong on One Directory Matter?

Because Google builds confidence in your business by cross-checking how it appears across the web. When your name, address and phone number match everywhere, that's a consistent signal. When four directories show an old number and two show a suite that no longer exists, the picture gets fuzzy, and a fuzzy business is a less confident recommendation.

This is the least interesting work in local SEO and one of the highest-return.

What counts as a citation?

Any mention of your business name together with your phone number or address. Directory listings, chamber of commerce pages, industry association member lists, supplier "where to buy" pages, sponsorship pages from the youth league you back, local news mentions.

Not all of them are equal. A listing on a data aggregator that feeds dozens of smaller directories is worth more than the thirtieth generic directory nobody visits. The goal is accuracy at the sources that matter, not volume for its own sake.

Where do the inconsistencies come from?

Usually history. You moved. You changed phone systems. You added a tracking number for a campaign and it propagated. A previous marketing company set up listings you never knew about and can't log into. Someone spelled the business name with "and" instead of "&."

Every one of those becomes a slightly different version of you living somewhere on the internet. They don't disappear on their own, and they don't announce themselves. You find them by looking.

Does a tracking phone number break this?

Not if it's set up properly. Use dynamic number insertion so that visitors see a tracking number while the underlying code keeps your real number in the markup and schema — and keep the real number on your Google Business Profile.

What causes problems is hard-coding a different tracking number in each directory. That's the worst possible version: your call data gets split across sources and your citations disagree with each other at the same time.

What about Bing, Apple Maps and the rest?

They matter more than most people assume, because they're not primarily used through their own websites. Apple Maps is what an iPhone opens for directions, and Siri draws from it. Bing Places feeds Windows and parts of Microsoft's assistant tooling.

They're free, they take an hour combined, and almost nobody in most Omaha categories has claimed them. That's a rare thing in local SEO: real ground available for the cost of an afternoon.

What separates a city page from a doorway page?

Substance. A doorway page is the same content with the city name swapped, built purely to catch a search. Google's spam policies name this explicitly, and pages like it get filtered or ignored — often taking some trust with them.

A real city page has things only true of that place: jobs you actually did there with photos, neighborhoods you cover, permitting or code specifics, typical response time from your location, a review from a customer in that city. If you can swap the city name and the page still reads correctly, it isn't a city page.

Is it better to have five great pages or thirty thin ones?

Five, without much argument. Thirty thin pages spread your authority across pages that can't win anything, give Google more low-quality material to judge you by, and create a maintenance burden nobody keeps up with.

Start with the cities where you already have work to show. Add more as you earn the material. A city page built after your tenth job in Gretna will outperform one built before your first.

Seen Too Often

What Are the Most Common Local SEO Mistakes in Omaha?

These aren't exotic. Every one of them is costing somebody in this metro map pack positions right now.

Mistake 01

Keyword stuffing the business name

Changing your listing to "Smith Plumbing — Best Omaha Emergency Plumber" is a direct policy violation. It sometimes works briefly, competitors report it, and suspended listings are far harder to recover than slow ones.

Fix: your real business name, exactly as it appears on your truck and paperwork.
Mistake 02

A virtual office or mailbox address

Renting an address to appear closer to downtown is the fastest way to lose the listing entirely. Google removes them, competitors report them, and there's no appeal that ends well.

Fix: set up as a service-area business and hide the address properly.
Mistake 03

Claiming the profile and abandoning it

A listing that hasn't had a photo, post or answered question in two years reads as a dormant business. Meanwhile the competitor posting monthly looks like the safer call.

Fix: one photo and one post a month. Fifteen minutes.
Mistake 04

Ignoring reviews entirely

Not asking, and not replying. Both are visible. A listing with twelve reviews and no owner responses tells a prospect exactly how much attention they should expect after paying you.

Fix: ask at job completion, reply to every review within a week.
Mistake 05

Buying reviews or trading them

Detectable, prohibited, and increasingly investigated by regulators, not just platforms. The downside is losing the listing and the trust; the upside is a temporary bump.

Fix: fix the thing that's stopping happy customers from leaving reviews.
Mistake 06

One page for the entire metro

A single page listing fifteen suburbs in a footer paragraph ranks for none of them. It reads as filler to a person and provides nothing specific for a search engine to match.

Fix: fewer, better pages where you have real material.
Mistake 07

Checking rankings from the office

You search your term, see yourself at the top, and conclude you're winning. Google personalizes by location and history — you're seeing the most flattering possible version of your results.

Fix: check from multiple points across the metro, logged out.
Mistake 08

Treating the profile and the site as separate

The profile links to the website and Google reads both. A neglected site undermines a good profile, and a great site can't fix a half-empty listing. They're one system.

Fix: audit both together, and make them agree on every fact.
